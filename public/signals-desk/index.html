The argument in brief

  1. Lebanese control is made conditional on disarmament.
  2. The framework supplies no deadline for complete Israeli withdrawal.
  3. Reconstruction and return remain exposed to continued bombardment.

“Until then, Lebanon gets to raise the flag; Israel gets to decide whether anyone can live beneath it.”

Karim Salam · 7 September 2026
Read the full 7 September analysis

In Kafr Rumman, Israeli strikes killed at least 12 people on September 7, including two children and a paramedic. Israel said it targeted people transferring weapons. One strike hit a residential building; another hit a vehicle. Lebanon's sovereignty is being negotiated while Israel continues deciding where Lebanese life can safely exist.

Lebanon is being asked to demonstrate that it deserves control of its own territory, while Israel retains the military means to postpone the handover. That is the governing danger: a process advertised as ending occupation can become a procedure for administering it.

The agreements are different. The November 2024 ceasefire sought implementation of Resolution 1701, with Lebanese army deployment and Israeli withdrawal coordinated within sixty days under an American-led mechanism involving France. The June 19, 2026 ceasefire, arranged by Washington and Qatar with Iranian assistance, was a renewed halt to fighting. The June 26 trilateral framework is an interim political and security arrangement that explicitly envisages negotiating a comprehensive peace agreement. A peace treaty is therefore an objective of the talks, not an accomplished fact. Its immediate machinery is the transfer of mutually agreed "pilot zones" to Lebanese forces.

That machinery changes the burden. Under the June framework, reconstruction and civilian return in those zones follow verified disarmament; American assistance depends on demonstrated Lebanese performance. Unlike the 2024 arrangement, it supplies no deadline for complete Israeli withdrawal. The text preserves both states' right of self-defence under international law. But formal symmetry conceals unequal power. Israel translates its own threat assessment into bombardment. Lebanon petitions the governments expected to restrain it.

The distinction is visible in Zawtar al-Gharbiyah, where Prime Minister Nawaf Salam raised the Lebanese flag on July 22. The army has cleared roads, removed unexploded ordnance and supplied water. That is real public work, not theatre. Yet Israeli forces remain immediately nearby, and parts of the village remain inaccessible. A flag-raising becomes sovereignty theatre when useful Lebanese administration is presented as proof that the foreign military veto has disappeared.

The strongest defence of the arrangement deserves a plain hearing: Hezbollah remains armed, and phased, verified handovers could protect residents on both sides while rebuilding state authority. Today, Israel's military reported that a Hezbollah gunman wounded two soldiers near Beaufort Castle before Israeli forces killed him; it also reported two explosive drones aimed at troops near Ali Taher. These are substantive security claims. The cabinet's March ban on Hezbollah's military activity asserted a necessary principle: the government, not an Iran-backed party, should decide whether Lebanon enters a war.

But an examination cannot produce sovereignty while the examiner retains the power to destroy the conditions for passing. Israel claimed operational control of Ali Taher on September 3; subsequent strikes hit surrounding towns where residents had returned after June. Remaining resistance supplies the argument for further operations, which make return harder and occupation more durable. The ratchet does not require an imaginary threat. It requires a real threat and no effective enforcement of the limits on Israel's response.

Today Joseph Aoun sought French support for extending UNIFIL, whose mandate is due to end on December 31, 2026, and welcomed plans for a French-Italian alternative. These efforts matter. But a replacement mission must be judged by whether it constrains violations, not by how many foreigners supervise Lebanese compliance. Washington cannot establish a credible Lebanese monopoly of force merely by financing the army expected to operate beneath Israeli aircraft.

More than 340,000 people remain displaced as schools prepare to reopen on September 15. Their children need classrooms that are not shelters. In Nabatieh al-Fawqa, Israel destroyed the evacuated Ghandour Hospital on September 6. Reconstruction cannot outrun a military system that keeps demolishing the conditions of return. Without a binding withdrawal timetable and consequences for attacks, temporary security measures can harden into permanent dispossession. Until then, Lebanon gets to raise the flag; Israel gets to decide whether anyone can live beneath it.
